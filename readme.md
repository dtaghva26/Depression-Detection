Depression is a common but serious mental health condition. It affects how a person feels, thinks, and gets through daily life. It is more than ordinary sadness or a few bad days. It usually shows up as a persistent low mood, a loss of interest or pleasure in things that used to matter, and a set of physical and cognitive symptoms: changes in sleep and appetite, fatigue, trouble concentrating, and feelings of worthlessness or guilt. Its causes are rarely simple. Genetics, brain chemistry, chronic stress, trauma, illness, and social isolation can all contribute, often in combination. Depression can be debilitating, but it is also highly treatable. Psychotherapy (especially cognitive behavioural therapy), medication, exercise, social support, or a mix of these help most people feel significantly better. Reducing the stigma around depression matters, because many people suffer quietly for a long time before they seek the help that could change their lives.

## Why this project

University students face a mix of pressures that put them at higher risk of depression: academic workload, financial strain, living away from family, relationship difficulties and health problems. Depression is treatable, but many students go years without asking for help, and it often goes unnoticed until it affects their grades, health or safety. We wanted to see whether simple information a university might already collect (demographics, semester, CGPA, living situation, family background, social support and health status) could flag students who may be at risk, so that support services could reach out earlier. We also wanted to see which factors are most closely linked to depression in this population. The goal is a screening aid that prompts a conversation, not a diagnostic tool.

## Outcomes

Using a dataset of 4,464 students (about 27% reporting depression), we built a reproducible scikit-learn pipeline that encodes the data inside the model, so test data never leaks into training. We compared a naive baseline with logistic regression, random forest and gradient boosting using stratified 5-fold cross-validation, focusing on F1 and ROC-AUC rather than accuracy because the classes are imbalanced. Gradient boosting performed best (cross-validated F1 ≈ 0.99, ROC-AUC ≈ 1.00), well ahead of the baseline (73% accuracy but F1 = 0).

The analysis also showed that these near-perfect scores come almost entirely from one feature, `Health_condition`. Every student with a "Critical" health condition reported depression, and none with a "Good" one did. With that feature removed, performance drops to a more realistic F1 ≈ 0.54 and ROC-AUC ≈ 0.74. Other factors, such as being single or lacking a friend circle, still carry some signal, but much less. So health status is the strongest marker of depression in this data, but it may partly reflect the outcome itself rather than predict it. Feature importance and SHAP analysis were used to make the model's decisions transparent. Any real-world use would need validation on independently collected data and careful thought about privacy and ethics.
